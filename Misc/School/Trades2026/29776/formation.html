Formation of Exoplanetary Systems

Overview

Other planetary systems are thought to form the same way ours did. Part of a molecular cloud collapses under its own gravity and flattens out into a spinning disk of gas and dust around a young star. This idea, called the nebular hypothesis, was first worked out to explain the Solar System, but scientists think it applies to other stars too. Telescopes have spotted young stars surrounded by exactly this kind of disk.

Core accretion

The main idea for how rocky and icy planets form is called core accretion. Dust grains in the disk bump into each other and stick together, slowly building up into bigger chunks called planetesimals, and eventually into full planet cores. If a core grows big enough while gas is still around, it can quickly pull in a thick layer of hydrogen and helium, becoming a gas giant. This idea explains how both ice giants and gas giants likely form, though it may not explain how the biggest gas giants form fast enough before the gas in the disk runs out.

Disk instability

A different idea, called disk instability, suggests that a clump of gas in a cool, heavy disk can collapse under its own gravity and form a giant planet directly, without needing to slowly build up a core first. This seems to work best far from the star, where the disk is colder. It may explain some giant planets found on wide orbits that core accretion struggles to account for.

Planet migration

Many giant planets seem to have formed much farther from their star than where they sit today. Interactions with leftover gas and dust in the disk, or the gravity of other planets, can push a planet inward or outward over time. This process, called migration, is the best explanation for hot Jupiters, gas giants that likely formed far out and then drifted inward to orbits closer to their star than Mercury is to the Sun.

Watching planets form

Some of the best evidence for these ideas comes from young systems caught in the act of building planets. The star PDS 70, about 370 light-years away, has two giant planets, PDS 70b and PDS 70c, still sitting inside a gap they carved into their disk. These were the first planets ever photographed while they were still forming.

Rogue planet formation

Not every planet stays attached to a star. Some form around a star as normal, then get thrown out of their system entirely after a close encounter with another planet. Others might form on their own, collapsing straight from a small cloud fragment much like a star does, just without enough mass to start burning as one. Both of these are thought to explain the rogue, free-floating planets drifting through the galaxy.

Future evolution

What happens to a planetary system long-term depends heavily on its star. As a star ages and swells into a red giant, planets orbiting close to it, like hot Jupiters, are expected to be swallowed whole. Some, like WASP-12b, are already being dragged slowly inward by tidal forces well before their star even reaches that stage. Planets on wider orbits have a much better chance of surviving their star's later life, similar to how the outer Solar System is expected to outlast the Sun's red giant phase.

Galactic context

Surveys from missions like Kepler suggest planets are common rather than rare, with an estimated average of at least one planet per star across the Milky Way. That means our galaxy alone could hold tens of billions of planets. Most of these have never been seen directly. Scientists work out that they likely exist just by looking at how often planets turn up in the systems that have been studied closely.
