Formation of Exoplanetary Systems

Overview

Planetary systems form the same way our own did, through the gravitational collapse of a fragment of a molecular cloud, which flattens into a spinning protoplanetary disk of gas and dust around a young star. The nebular hypothesis that explains the Solar System's formation is thought to apply generally to other stars too, and observations of young stars across the galaxy show discs of exactly the kind the hypothesis predicts.

Core accretion

The leading model for forming rocky and icy planets is core accretion, in which dust grains in the disk collide and stick together, gradually building up planetesimals and eventually full planetary cores. If a core grows large enough while gas is still present in the disk, it can rapidly pull in a thick hydrogen and helium envelope, forming a gas giant. This process is thought to explain the formation of ice giants and gas giants alike, though it may struggle to build the largest gas giants quickly enough before their disk's gas disperses.

Disk instability

An alternative model, disk instability, proposes that clumps of gas in an unstable, cool, and massive disk can collapse directly under their own gravity to form a giant planet, skipping the slower core-building stage entirely. This process is thought to work best far from the host star, where the disk is cooler, and may explain some giant planets found on very wide orbits that core accretion struggles to account for.

Planet migration

Many giant planets appear to have formed farther from their star than where they are found today, then migrated inward or outward through interactions with the remaining gas and dust in the disk, or gravitational tugs from other planets. This migration is the leading explanation for hot Jupiters, gas giants that likely formed beyond the frost line before spiralling inward to orbits closer to their star than Mercury is to the Sun.

Watching planets form

Direct evidence for these processes has come from young systems still in the act of forming planets. The star PDS 70, about 370 light-years away, hosts two giant planets, PDS 70b and PDS 70c, caught still embedded within a gap they have carved into their parent disk, the first planets ever directly imaged while still forming.

Rogue planet formation

Not every planet-sized object ends up bound to a star. Some form around a star before being flung out of their system entirely by gravitational encounters with other planets, while others may collapse directly from a cloud fragment too small to ignite as a star, similar to how a star forms but on a smaller scale. Both pathways are thought to contribute to the population of rogue, free-floating planets drifting through the galaxy.

Future evolution

A planetary system's fate is tied closely to its star. As a host star ages and expands into a red giant, close-in planets like hot Jupiters are expected to be engulfed entirely, and tidal forces are already dragging some, such as WASP-12b, slowly inward toward their star well before that stage is reached. Planets on wider orbits are more likely to survive their star's evolution, much as the outer Solar System is expected to outlast the Sun's red giant phase.

Galactic context

Statistical surveys from missions like Kepler suggest that planets are the rule rather than the exception, with an estimated average of at least one planet per star across the Milky Way. This implies our galaxy alone may host tens of billions of planets, the large majority of which have never been directly observed and are inferred only from the frequency of the systems that have been studied closely.
