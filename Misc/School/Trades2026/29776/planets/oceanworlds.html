Ocean Worlds

Overview

Ocean worlds are exoplanets thought to be covered by a deep, global layer of liquid water, far deeper than any ocean on Earth. A subclass known as hycean worlds, a term combining "hydrogen" and "ocean" proposed in 2021, describes planets with a global ocean beneath a thick hydrogen-rich atmosphere, a combination with no equivalent in the Solar System.

Why they might exist

Many known exoplanets sit in the size range between rocky super-Earths and gas-rich mini-Neptunes, and models suggest some of these could be water-rich worlds where ice and rock in the interior are covered by a deep water layer, in turn topped by a hydrogen or steam atmosphere depending on temperature. Because water is abundant in protoplanetary disks, especially beyond the frost line, planets migrating inward from icier regions could plausibly deliver enough water to form such an ocean.

Possible conditions

Depending on temperature and atmospheric pressure, an ocean world's surface conditions could range from a temperate liquid ocean to a scalding layer of supercritical water and steam with no clear boundary between liquid and gas at all. The hycean concept specifically proposes conditions mild enough at the ocean surface to potentially support life, despite the crushing pressures and hydrogen atmosphere above it.

Notable examples

K2-18 b, a mini-Neptune-sized planet in its star's habitable zone, has been proposed as a candidate hycean world, and observations with the James Webb Space Telescope have detected water vapour and carbon-bearing molecules in its atmosphere. TOI-1452 b, orbiting a red dwarf star, has also been proposed as a possible ocean world candidate based on its size and estimated density.

Uncertainty

No ocean world has been directly confirmed, since current telescopes cannot see through a planet's atmosphere to its surface or interior. Bulk density alone is often consistent with multiple different interior structures, so claims of ocean worlds rely on combining density estimates with atmospheric composition and climate models rather than direct observation of liquid water.

Detection & Study

Ocean world candidates are first flagged by their size and mass from transit and radial velocity measurements, then studied further with transmission spectroscopy, which analyses starlight filtered through a planet's atmosphere during a transit to identify the gases present. This technique, powered largely by the James Webb Space Telescope, is the main tool driving current hycean world research.
