Lava Worlds

Overview

Lava worlds are rocky exoplanets that orbit so close to their star that part or all of their surface is thought to be melted. Most known examples are ultra-short-period planets, finishing a full orbit in under a day, close enough that starlight alone can heat their surface past the point where most rock melts.

Formation

Lava worlds are thought to start as rocky planets, or as the leftover cores of former mini-Neptunes, before drifting inward to an extremely close orbit. Some may have simply formed close to their star from the start. Any atmosphere they once had would have been stripped away quickly at such close range, leaving a bare rocky surface facing straight into the starlight.

Physical characteristics

A lava world that's locked in place, always facing its star, shows an extreme difference between its permanently lit side, hot enough to melt rock, and its dark side, cool enough for rock vapour to turn back into liquid and rain down as it drifts around. This creates one of the biggest climate differences known on any planet.

Atmosphere

Instead of a normal gas atmosphere, some lava worlds might have a thin haze made of vaporised rock, rising off the molten dayside and turning back into liquid once it drifts to cooler areas. Observations of the lava world K2-141 b suggest winds could carry this rock vapour toward the dark side, where models predict it could fall as mineral rain.

Notable examples

Janssen (55 Cancri e), one of the best-studied lava worlds, has a dayside temperature of around 2,000°C and might have a surface of molten or even liquid rock. Some earlier ideas suggested its interior could be rich in carbon, though this is still debated. CoRoT-7 b, found in 2009, was the first rocky exoplanet confirmed outside the Solar System, and is thought to have a similarly molten dayside.

Detection

Lava worlds are usually found through the transit method. Their extreme heat can sometimes be measured directly by picking up infrared light coming from the planet itself as it passes behind its star, a technique called secondary eclipse spectroscopy. This has let astronomers roughly map out temperature differences across a lava world's surface without ever seeing it directly.
