Mini-Neptunes

Overview

Mini-Neptunes, sometimes called gas dwarfs, are exoplanets smaller than Neptune but still big enough to hold onto a thick layer of hydrogen and helium gas over a rocky or icy core. They sit at the larger end of the size range that overlaps with super-Earths, and telling the two apart usually comes down to whether a thick gas layer is present.

Formation

Mini-Neptunes are thought to form like bigger gas giants, with a solid core pulling in gas from the protoplanetary disk around it, but they stop growing before they can pull in enough to become a full ice giant. Their smaller size compared to Neptune or Uranus might come down to less gas being nearby, or less time before the disk's gas ran out.

Physical characteristics

Even though a mini-Neptune isn't huge overall, its thick hydrogen layer can make it look much bigger than a rocky planet with a similar mass, since gas takes up far more space than rock does. This means size alone can't tell you what a planet is made of. Scientists usually need a mass measurement from radial velocity to work out roughly how much of a mini-Neptune is gas and how much is solid core.

Atmosphere

Mini-Neptune atmospheres are mostly hydrogen and helium, often covered in clouds or haze that make it hard for telescopes to see deeper down or work out what the core is made of. Some, like Enaiposha (GJ 1214 b), show almost no clear features in their light, a sign that thick haze or high clouds might be blocking the view.

Notable examples

Enaiposha (GJ 1214 b) was one of the first mini-Neptunes studied closely, and it's still used as a standard example of the type. K2-18 b, a mini-Neptune sitting in its star's habitable zone, drew a lot of attention after scientists spotted water vapour in its atmosphere, along with a disputed 2023 claim of a possible sign of life. That claim is still being debated.

Detection

Mini-Neptunes are found the same way as most other exoplanets, mostly through transits, and they turned out to be one of the most common planet sizes found by the Kepler mission, even though there's no planet quite like this in our own Solar System. Some scientists think this gap in our system, sometimes called the "radius valley", happens because smaller mini-Neptunes lose their gas over time and turn into super-Earths instead.
