Hot Jupiters

Overview

Hot Jupiters are gas giant exoplanets, about the same size and mass as Jupiter, orbiting extremely close to their star, often closer than Mercury is to the Sun. The first exoplanet ever found around a star like the Sun, Dimidium (51 Pegasi b) in 1995, was a hot Jupiter. This type of planet is still one of the easiest kinds to spot.

Formation

Hot Jupiters are thought to form far from their star, past the frost line where icy material helps build a large core, and then drift inward over time. This can happen through interactions with their protoplanetary disk or a gravitational pull from other planets. Few scientists think a gas giant could form in place so close to its star, since the heat there would stop a big enough core from coming together in time.

Physical characteristics

Like Jupiter, hot Jupiters are made mostly of hydrogen and helium. Their extreme heat puffs many of them up far bigger than Jupiter, even when they weigh about the same or less. Many are also locked in place facing their star, always showing the same side to it, much like how the Moon always shows the same side to Earth.

Atmosphere & Weather

Daytime temperatures on a hot Jupiter can top 1,000°C, hot enough to turn metals like iron into gas and boil part of the planet's atmosphere away into space. Strong winds are thought to carry heat from the permanently lit side to the dark side. Some hot Jupiters, like WASP-76 b, have even been found to rain molten iron on their dark side as the metal gas cools down and turns back into liquid.

Notable examples

WASP-12 b is one of the most extreme hot Jupiters known. Its star's gravity has stretched it into an egg shape, and it's slowly spiralling inward, likely to be swallowed within a few million years. HD 209458 b, nicknamed Osiris, was the first exoplanet ever seen passing in front of its star, and the first found to have an atmosphere that's slowly leaking away into space.

Detection

Hot Jupiters are usually found with the transit method, since their large size and close orbit cause a strong, frequent dip in starlight. They can also be found with the radial velocity method, since their mass and closeness cause a bigger wobble in their star than smaller planets would. This made hot Jupiters the first type of exoplanet found in large numbers, even though they turned out to be far less common overall than smaller, more distant planets.
