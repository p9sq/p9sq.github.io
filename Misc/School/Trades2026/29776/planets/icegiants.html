Exo-Ice Giants

Overview

Exo-ice giants are exoplanets comparable in size and composition to Uranus and Neptune, with a deep mantle of water, ammonia, and methane surrounding a rocky core, topped by a relatively thin hydrogen and helium atmosphere. They are less common among confirmed exoplanets than smaller super-Earths and mini-Neptunes, particularly on close orbits.

Formation

Like Uranus and Neptune, exo-ice giants are thought to form from a core that grew large enough to capture a modest gas envelope, but not quickly or massively enough to become a full gas giant like Jupiter or Saturn before their disk's gas dispersed. Many are found on wider orbits than hot Jupiters, though some warm and even hot examples have been discovered close to their star.

The Neptune desert

Astronomers have noted a scarcity of Neptune-sized planets on very close, hot orbits compared to other sizes, a pattern nicknamed the "Neptune desert". The leading explanation is that intense radiation this close to a star can strip away a Neptune-mass planet's atmosphere entirely, either destroying it or shrinking it down into a smaller, rocky super-Earth-sized remnant over time.

Atmosphere

Exo-ice giant atmospheres are expected to be dominated by hydrogen and helium with traces of methane, similar to Uranus and Neptune, though the exact composition of any given example is difficult to confirm without detailed spectroscopy. Warmer exo-ice giants closer to their star may lose lighter atmospheric gases over time, slowly changing their composition compared to more distant, colder examples.

Notable examples

GJ 436 b, a warm Neptune-sized planet, was found to have an unusual comet-like tail of escaping hydrogen gas, evidence of ongoing atmospheric loss driven by its star's radiation. HAT-P-11b is another well-studied Neptune-sized exoplanet, notable for a detection of water vapour in its atmosphere despite its relatively small size.

Detection

Exo-ice giants are found using the same transit and radial velocity methods as other exoplanets, with their intermediate size between rocky planets and true gas giants placing them within reach of most major exoplanet-hunting missions. Studying their atmospheres in detail generally requires a bright, nearby host star, since a smaller planet produces a fainter transit signal to analyse.
