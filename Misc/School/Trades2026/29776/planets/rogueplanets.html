Rogue Planets

Overview

Rogue planets, also called free-floating planets, are planetary-mass objects that drift through space without being gravitationally bound to any star. Because they emit no reflected starlight and produce little of their own, they are among the hardest kinds of planet to detect, and their true numbers across the galaxy remain highly uncertain.

How they form

Rogue planets are thought to form through two main pathways: some form around a star like any other planet before being ejected entirely by a close gravitational encounter with another planet or passing star, while others may collapse directly from a small cloud fragment, similar to how a star forms but without enough mass to ignite fusion. Both pathways are thought to contribute to the overall population, though their relative importance is still debated.

Detection

Since rogue planets have no host star to wobble or dim, they are usually found through gravitational microlensing, in which a rogue planet's gravity briefly bends and brightens the light of a much more distant background star as it passes in front of it from Earth's point of view. More recently, sensitive infrared surveys have also directly imaged faint, young rogue planets glowing with leftover heat from their formation.

Notable discoveries

In 2023, the James Webb Space Telescope identified around 40 pairs of free-floating, Jupiter-mass objects in the Orion Nebula, nicknamed JuMBOs, whose existence as isolated pairs is difficult to explain through standard planet formation or ejection alone. Microlensing surveys such as OGLE have also reported evidence for a substantial population of Earth to Jupiter-mass rogue planets scattered throughout the galaxy.

How many are there?

Some early microlensing studies suggested rogue planets could outnumber ordinary stars in the Milky Way, potentially by a wide margin, though later surveys with better statistics have generally found smaller, though still significant, numbers. The uncertainty largely comes down to how rare short, hard-to-catch microlensing events are, and how many rogue planets are too faint or too distant for current instruments to find at all.

Habitability speculation

Without a star, a rogue planet's surface would receive essentially no external heat, but some scientists have speculated that larger rogue planets with substantial internal heat, a thick insulating atmosphere, or a subsurface ocean kept liquid by internal heating could theoretically remain habitable in principle, similar to how some icy moons in the Solar System are thought to host subsurface oceans far from the Sun's warmth.
