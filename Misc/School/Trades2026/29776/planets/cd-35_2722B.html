CD-35 2722 B's Exosatellite

Overview

In July 2026, astronomers reported strong evidence for a Jupiter-mass object orbiting CD-35 2722 B, a brown dwarf about 71 light-years from Earth, in a paper led by Kevin Hoy and published in Nature. If confirmed, it would be the first moon-like object ever detected outside the Solar System, though its unusual setup has led astronomers to debate what to actually call it.

The system

At the centre of the system is CD-35 2722 A, a young star with roughly half the mass of the Sun. Orbiting it is CD-35 2722 B, a brown dwarf with around 30 times the mass of Jupiter, first discovered through direct imaging in 2009 and confirmed as a genuine companion in 2011. Brown dwarfs sit in an unusual middle ground between planets and stars, too massive to be a planet but not massive enough to sustain the hydrogen fusion that powers true stars.

The new discovery

Using the radial velocity method, the same technique first used to discover an exoplanet around a Sun-like star in 1995, astronomers analysed spectra of CD-35 2722 B itself, collected with the CRIRES+ instrument on ESO's Very Large Telescope. They found a periodic signal consistent with at least one object of roughly Jupiter's mass orbiting the brown dwarf, rather than the brown dwarf orbiting its star.

Why it's hard to classify

In the Solar System, the categories are simple: planets orbit the Sun, and moons orbit planets. The CD-35 2722 system blurs this completely, since the newly found object is massive enough to be called a planet in its own right, yet it does not orbit a star directly, it orbits a brown dwarf that itself orbits a star. Researchers have proposed calling such an object an "exosatellite" rather than an exomoon, since the term exomoon was originally defined for objects orbiting a planet, not a brown dwarf.

Still being confirmed

Despite the excitement, the discovery team has been careful to describe this as strong evidence rather than a final confirmation. Previous exomoon candidates, including proposed moons around Kepler-1625 b and Kepler-1708 b, were also announced with initial confidence before running into long-running scientific debate, and some astronomers argue the CD-35 2722 object should simply be described as a planet rather than any kind of moon. Confirming its exact nature will likely require further observations and more detailed modelling of how such a massive satellite could have formed and remained stable in orbit.

Why it matters

Out of more than 6,000 confirmed exoplanets, no exomoon of any kind has ever been confidently confirmed, despite years of searching, so even a single well-supported example would be a major milestone. Beyond the naming debate, a confirmed satellite like this could offer valuable insight into how planet-sized objects form and evolve around companions that are themselves not quite full stars.
