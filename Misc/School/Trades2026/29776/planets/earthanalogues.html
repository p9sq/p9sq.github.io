Earth Analogs

Overview

Earth analogues are rocky exoplanets close to Earth's size that orbit inside their star's habitable zone, the range of distances where temperatures could allow liquid water to sit on a planet's surface. The name only describes a planet's size and orbit. Confirming an atmosphere, water, or anything else Earth-like is still far beyond what our technology can check for most known examples.

The habitable zone

A star's habitable zone is the range of distances where a rocky planet with the right atmosphere could keep liquid water on its surface. Too close and the water would boil away; too far and it would freeze. How wide this zone is, and where it sits, depends a lot on how hot and bright the star is. That means an Earth-sized planet could sit in the habitable zone at quite different distances depending on what kind of star it orbits.

Formation

Earth analogues are thought to form the same way Earth did, by slowly building up from smaller rocky chunks in the inner part of a protoplanetary disk, inside the frost line where ice can't stay solid. Whether a planet like this ends up with enough water for oceans likely depends on how much icy material got delivered from farther out in the system, whether by drifting rocky chunks, comets, or something else.

Notable examples

Proxima Centauri b, orbiting the closest star to the Sun, is the nearest known possibly rocky exoplanet sitting in a habitable zone, though its red dwarf star's frequent flares raise doubts about whether an atmosphere could survive there. The TRAPPIST-1 system, found in 2017, has seven roughly Earth-sized planets, four of which sit in or near the habitable zone, making it one of the most studied systems when it comes to possible habitability. Kepler-186 f, announced in 2014, was the first Earth-sized planet confirmed in another star's habitable zone.

Detection challenges

Earth-sized planets are some of the hardest to find, since they only cause a tiny dip in starlight or a tiny wobble in their star, compared to bigger planets. Earth analogues in a habitable zone are even harder, since that zone usually sits on a wider, slower orbit than the close-in planets that short surveys pick up most easily. Confirming one can take years of steady watching.

Studying atmospheres

Working out whether an Earth analogue actually has an atmosphere, let alone water or signs of life, needs a technique called transmission spectroscopy, done during a transit. The James Webb Space Telescope has made this method far more sensitive, but it still works best for planets orbiting small, dim red dwarf stars, where the atmospheric signal is easier to pick up. Future telescopes are being built specifically to try this kind of study on Sun-like stars too.
