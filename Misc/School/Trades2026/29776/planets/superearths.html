Super-Earths

Overview

Super-Earths are exoplanets with a mass greater than Earth's but well below that of ice giants like Uranus and Neptune, roughly between 1 and 10 Earth masses. The name refers only to size and mass, not habitability, and a super-Earth may or may not be rocky, since the category overlaps with smaller, gas-rich mini-Neptunes at its upper end.

Formation

Super-Earths are thought to form the same way as Earth, through the gradual accretion of planetesimals in a protoplanetary disk, but either start with more building material available or avoid growing large enough to pull in a thick gas envelope before their disk's gas disperses. Many are found orbiting much closer to their star than Earth does to the Sun, likely a result of some migration inward during or shortly after formation.

Physical characteristics

A rocky super-Earth is expected to have stronger surface gravity than Earth, since gravity increases with mass faster than radius does for similar rocky compositions, meaning even a "flatter" landscape could feel considerably heavier to stand on. Some super-Earths may also retain enough internal heat and gravity to sustain plate tectonics for far longer than Earth is expected to.

Composition

Not every super-Earth is rocky. Some appear to be made largely of water or other volatile ices, sometimes called ocean planets, while others may be stripped-down cores of former mini-Neptunes that lost their gas envelope to intense radiation from a close-in orbit. This variety makes bulk composition difficult to determine from mass and radius alone.

Notable examples

LHS 1140 b is a well-studied rocky super-Earth orbiting within its star's habitable zone, considered one of the more promising nearby targets for atmospheric study. Kepler-452 b, sometimes described as one of Earth's closest known cousins in size, orbits a Sun-like star at a distance similar to Earth's, though its exact composition remains uncertain.

Detection

Super-Earths are detected through the same main techniques as other exoplanets, transits and radial velocity, but their smaller size compared to gas giants makes them harder to find, requiring more sensitive instruments. Missions like Kepler, TESS, and ground-based radial velocity surveys have found that super-Earths are among the most common types of planet in the galaxy, far outnumbering Jupiter-sized worlds.
