Overview of Exoplanets

An exoplanet is a planet that orbits a star other than the Sun. The first exoplanets were found in 1992, orbiting a pulsar named Lich (PSR B1257+12). The first exoplanet found orbiting a star like the Sun, Dimidium (51 Pegasi b), was discovered in 1995. This discovery later won the astronomers who found it the 2019 Nobel Prize in Physics. Since then, missions like Kepler and TESS have helped push the number of confirmed exoplanets past 6,000. Studies also suggest that, on average, every star in the Milky Way has at least one planet. Exoplanets don't fit into a strict set of groups the way the Solar System's planets do. Instead, they get grouped loosely by size, what they're made of, and how they orbit:
  • Hot Jupiters
  • Super-Earths
  • Mini-Neptunes
  • Ocean Worlds
These four groups cover most of the planets found so far, since they sit close to their star and are easier to spot. Other groups cover planets with more extreme conditions or unusual origins:
  • Lava Worlds
  • Exo-Ice Giants
  • Rogue Planets
  • Earth Analogs
Astronomers mainly use four methods to find exoplanets. The radial velocity method picks up the tiny wobble a planet's gravity causes in its star. The transit method looks for a small dip in starlight when a planet passes in front of its star. Direct imaging takes a photo of a planet's own light, separately from its star's light. Gravitational microlensing spots a brief flash of extra brightness caused by a planet's gravity bending light from a star far behind it. Astronomers have also spotted belts of leftover rock and ice around other stars, similar to the Solar System's asteroid belt and Kuiper belt. Many planetary systems are also thought to have an Oort cloud of their own, a shell of icy objects too far out to see directly. In 2026, astronomers reported strong evidence for a Jupiter-sized object, similar to a moon, orbiting the brown dwarf CD-35 2722 B. This could be a first step toward confirming a moon outside the Solar System. Read more about this unusual system.
