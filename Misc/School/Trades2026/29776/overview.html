Overview of Exoplanets

An exoplanet is any planet that orbits a star other than the Sun. The first confirmed exoplanets were found in 1992 orbiting a pulsar named PSR B1257+12, and the first exoplanet found orbiting a Sun-like star, 51 Pegasi b, followed in 1995, a discovery that later won its astronomers the 2019 Nobel Prize in Physics. Since then, missions like Kepler and TESS have pushed the confirmed count past 6,000, and statistical surveys suggest there is on average at least one planet for every star in the Milky Way. Exoplanets are grouped into rough categories based on size, composition, and orbit, rather than a strict class system like the Solar System's terrestrial and giant planets:
  • Hot Jupiters
  • Super-Earths
  • Mini-Neptunes
  • Ocean Worlds
These four categories make up most of the close-in, easily detected planets found so far. Further categories describe planets defined by more extreme conditions or unusual origins:
  • Lava Worlds
  • Exo-Ice Giants
  • Rogue Planets
  • Earth Analogs
Exoplanets are found mainly through four techniques: the radial velocity method, which detects the tiny wobble a planet's gravity induces in its host star; the transit method, which measures the dip in starlight as a planet passes in front of its star; direct imaging, which photographs a planet's own light separately from its star; and gravitational microlensing, which catches the brief brightening caused by a planet's gravity bending light from a background star. Astronomers have also found belts of leftover debris around other stars, similar to our own asteroid belt and Kuiper belt, and suspect that many planetary systems may have Oort cloud-like reservoirs of icy bodies too far out to detect directly. In 2026, astronomers reported strong evidence for a Jupiter-mass exosatellite, an object like a moon, orbiting the brown dwarf CD-35 2722 B, a possible first step toward confirming a moon beyond the Solar System.
