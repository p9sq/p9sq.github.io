Debris Disks

Overview

A debris disk is a ring or belt of dust, rocky fragments, and icy bodies left orbiting a mature star once its original protoplanetary disk's gas has dispersed and planet formation has mostly finished. They are the exoplanetary equivalent of our own Kuiper Belt, a reservoir of leftover icy planetesimals beyond Neptune that never became part of a planet.

How they're detected

Debris disks are usually found through the excess infrared light they give off, as starlight warms the dust grains within them, making the disk glow at wavelengths a star alone would not produce. Some of the brightest and most studied debris disks have been imaged directly, revealing structure not unlike Kuiper Belt debris, including narrow rings, warps, and gaps that hint at unseen planets shepherding the material.

Notable examples

The star Beta Pictoris hosts one of the best-studied debris disks, seen edge-on from Earth, with a warm inner belt and a colder outer belt bearing a striking resemblance to the Solar System's asteroid belt and Kuiper belt working together. Fomalhaut and Vega also host prominent debris disks, and observations of Fomalhaut's disk with the James Webb Space Telescope in 2023 revealed a complex, multi-ringed structure with an inner asteroid belt-like component as well.

Shepherd planets

Sharp edges, gaps, and warps in a debris disk are often used as indirect evidence for planets too faint to be seen directly. The Fomalhaut system's inner disk edge, for example, is thought to be maintained by the gravity of an unseen planet, similar to how Neptune's gravity governs the inner boundary of the Kuiper Belt in our own Solar System.

Disk evolution

Debris disks slowly grind themselves down over hundreds of millions of years, as collisions between larger bodies produce smaller and smaller fragments that are eventually blown away by stellar radiation or swept up by planets. Younger stars tend to have brighter, more massive debris disks than older ones, giving astronomers a rough timeline for how these belts fade as a planetary system ages.
