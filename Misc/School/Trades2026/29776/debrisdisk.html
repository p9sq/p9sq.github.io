Debris Disks

Overview

A debris disk is a ring or belt of dust, rock, and ice left over around a mature star, once the star's original protoplanetary disk has run out of gas and most planet-building has finished. It's the exoplanet version of our own Kuiper Belt, a stash of leftover icy chunks past Neptune that never became part of a planet.

How they're detected

Debris disks are usually spotted through the extra infrared light they give off, since starlight warms up the dust and makes it glow at wavelengths the star alone wouldn't produce. Some of the brightest debris disks have been photographed directly, showing rings, warps, and gaps much like the debris in our own Kuiper Belt. These shapes often hint at planets too faint to see directly, shepherding the material into place.

Notable examples

The star Beta Pictoris has one of the best-studied debris disks, seen edge-on from Earth. It has a warm inner belt and a colder outer belt, a setup that looks a lot like our own asteroid belt and Kuiper belt working together. Fomalhaut and Vega also have well-known debris disks. In 2023, the James Webb Space Telescope photographed Fomalhaut's disk in detail and found a complicated structure with several rings, including one that looks like an asteroid belt of its own.

Shepherd planets

Sharp edges, gaps, and warps in a debris disk are often used as clues that a planet is nearby, even one too faint to see. The sharp inner edge of Fomalhaut's disk, for example, is thought to be held in place by the gravity of an unseen planet, similar to how Neptune's gravity shapes the inner edge of our own Kuiper Belt.

Disk evolution

Debris disks slowly wear themselves down over hundreds of millions of years. Collisions between bigger chunks create smaller and smaller pieces, which eventually get blown away by starlight or swept up by planets. Younger stars tend to have brighter, heavier debris disks than older ones, which gives astronomers a rough way to guess how old a system is just from how faint its disk has become.
