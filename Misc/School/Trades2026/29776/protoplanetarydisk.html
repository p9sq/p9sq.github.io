Protoplanetary Disks

Overview

A protoplanetary disk is a spinning ring of gas and dust that surrounds a young star in its first few million years. It's the raw material that planets, moons, and smaller objects like asteroids are built from. Our own asteroid belt is a leftover piece of the Solar System's protoplanetary disk, made up of chunks of rock that never managed to join together into a full planet.

Structure

A protoplanetary disk is usually hottest and thinnest close to the star, and cooler and denser further out. Somewhere in between sits the frost line, the point where it gets cold enough for water and other gases to freeze solid. This line is thought to explain why rocky planets tend to form close to a star, while icier, gas-rich planets tend to form farther away.

Gaps and rings

Sharp images from telescopes like ALMA show that many protoplanetary disks aren't smooth at all. Instead, they have gaps and bright rings, likely carved out by planets forming and sweeping up material as they orbit. A famous image of the disk around the young star HL Tauri, taken in 2014, was one of the first to show this clearly, and it changed how astronomers picture planets coming together.

Disks caught forming planets

A small number of disks have been caught with planets still sitting inside them. The star PDS 70 has two confirmed giant planets, PDS 70b and PDS 70c, both photographed inside gaps in its disk. This gives astronomers a rare, direct look at planets partway through forming, rather than already finished.

Disk lifetimes

Protoplanetary disks don't last long compared to the age of a star, usually breaking up within 5 to 10 million years. The star's own light and wind blow the gas away, and forming planets soak up the rest. Once the gas is gone, no new gas giants can form, though rocky planets can keep building for tens of millions of years more using the leftover dust and rock.
