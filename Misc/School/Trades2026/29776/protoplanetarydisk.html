Protoplanetary Disks

Overview

A protoplanetary disk is a rotating disk of gas and dust that surrounds a young star in the first few million years after it forms, and is the raw material from which planets, moons, and smaller bodies like asteroids are built. Our own asteroid belt is a leftover remnant of the Solar System's protoplanetary disk, made of planetesimals that never managed to accrete into a full planet.

Structure

Protoplanetary disks are typically hottest and thinnest close to the star and cooler and denser further out, with a boundary called the frost line marking where it becomes cold enough for water and other volatile compounds to freeze solid. This boundary is thought to explain why rocky planets tend to form close to a star while icier, gas-rich planets tend to form further out.

Gaps and rings

High-resolution images from telescopes like ALMA have revealed that many protoplanetary disks are not smooth, but instead show concentric gaps and bright rings, thought to be carved out by forming planets sweeping up material along their orbits. The disk around the young star HL Tauri was one of the first to show this structure clearly, in an image released in 2014 that reshaped how astronomers picture planet formation in progress.

Disks caught forming planets

A small number of disks have been observed with planets still embedded inside them. The star PDS 70 hosts two confirmed giant planets, PDS 70b and PDS 70c, both directly imaged within gaps in their disk, giving astronomers a rare direct look at planets partway through their formation rather than fully formed.

Disk lifetimes

Protoplanetary disks do not last long in astronomical terms, typically dispersing within about 5 to 10 million years as gas is blown away by the host star's radiation and stellar wind, or is swept up by forming planets. Once the gas is gone, a disk can no longer form new gas giants, though rocky planet formation can continue for tens of millions of years afterwards using the leftover dust and planetesimals.
