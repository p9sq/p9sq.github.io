Exo-Oort Clouds

Overview

An exo-Oort cloud is the theorised equivalent, around another star, of our own Oort cloud, a distant, extremely faint shell of icy bodies far beyond any debris disk or planet. Unlike protoplanetary and debris disks, no exo-Oort cloud has ever been directly observed around another star, since the icy bodies involved would be far too small, cold, and distant to detect with current telescopes.

Why they're expected to exist

Astronomers expect other stars to have Oort cloud-like reservoirs because the same process thought to have built our own is a natural consequence of planet formation: icy planetesimals flung onto wide, looping orbits by gravitational encounters with young giant planets, rather than being ejected from the system entirely. Since giant planets and debris disks are both common around other stars, the raw ingredients for building an exo-Oort cloud are thought to be common too.

Indirect evidence

The strongest indirect evidence comes from long-period comets in our own Solar System, which arrive from roughly all directions on extremely elongated orbits, exactly what is expected if they originate from a roughly spherical, distant cloud rather than a flattened disk like the Kuiper Belt. If this same mechanism operates around other stars, similar comet populations should exist there too, even though no telescope can currently see that far into another star's system.

Interstellar objects

Some scientists have proposed that a fraction of the interstellar objects passing through the Solar System, such as 1I/'Oumuamua in 2017 and 2I/Borisov in 2019, may originally have been ejected from the Oort cloud-like reservoirs of other star systems, drifting through the galaxy for millions or billions of years before we happened to observe them.

A largely theoretical frontier

Because exo-Oort clouds cannot yet be observed directly, most of what is known about them comes from computer simulations of how icy bodies are scattered during a system's early history. Confirming their existence around another star would likely require either a very fortunate direct detection of an unusually large, nearby object or future telescopes far more sensitive than those available today.
