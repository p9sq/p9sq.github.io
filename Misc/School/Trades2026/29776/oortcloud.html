Exo-Oort Clouds

Overview

An exo-Oort cloud is what scientists think another star's version of our own Oort cloud would look like: a huge, extremely faint shell of icy objects sitting far beyond any debris disk or planet. Unlike protoplanetary disks and debris disks, no one has ever directly spotted an exo-Oort cloud around another star. The icy objects inside would be far too small, cold, and distant for any current telescope to see.

Why they're expected to exist

Scientists expect other stars to have their own Oort cloud-like shells because the same process that likely built ours should happen almost anywhere planets form. Young giant planets can fling icy chunks onto huge, looping orbits instead of throwing them out of the system completely. Since giant planets and debris disks both show up around plenty of other stars, the basic ingredients for building an exo-Oort cloud are likely common too.

Indirect evidence

The best evidence so far comes from long-period comets in our own Solar System. These comets arrive from almost every direction on extremely long, stretched-out orbits, exactly what you'd expect if they come from a roughly ball-shaped, distant cloud rather than a flat disk like the Kuiper Belt. If the same thing happens around other stars, similar comets should exist there too, even though no telescope can currently see that far into another star's system.

Interstellar objects

Some scientists think a few of the interstellar objects that have passed through the Solar System, such as 1I/'Oumuamua in 2017 and 2I/Borisov in 2019, may have originally come from the Oort cloud-like shell of another star system. If so, they could have been drifting through the galaxy for millions or even billions of years before we happened to spot them.

A largely theoretical frontier

Since no one can see an exo-Oort cloud directly, most of what scientists know comes from computer models of how icy objects get scattered early in a system's history. Confirming one exists around another star would likely need either a lucky direct sighting of an unusually large, nearby object, or future telescopes that can see much farther than what we have today.
