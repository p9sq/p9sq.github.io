Overview of the Solar System

The Solar System is the gravitationally bound system of the Sun and everything that orbits it, most prominently its eight planets, of which Earth is one. It formed about 4.6 billion years ago when a dense region of a molecular cloud collapsed, creating the Sun and a protoplanetary disc from which the planets and other bodies assembled. The Sun alone accounts for 99.86% of the Solar System's total mass. Closest to the Sun are the four terrestrial planets:
  • Mercury
  • Venus
  • Earth
  • Mars
These make up the inner Solar System and are dense, rocky worlds with few or no moons. Beyond them lies the asteroid belt, a region between Mars and Jupiter containing tens of thousands of rocky objects left over from the Solar System's formation. Further out sit the four giant planets:
  • Jupiter
  • Saturn
  • Uranus
  • Neptune
These together make up 99% of the mass orbiting the Sun. Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants made mostly of hydrogen and helium, while Uranus and Neptune are ice giants made largely of water, methane, and ammonia. All four have rings and multiple moons, though only Saturn's rings are easily visible from Earth. Beyond Neptune lies the Kuiper Belt, a doughnut-shaped region of icy bodies that includes the dwarf planet Pluto. Further still is the scattered disc and a population of extreme trans-Neptunian objects, including the dwarf planet Sedna. The outermost region, the theorised Oort cloud, is believed to hold up to a trillion icy objects and is thought to be the source of long-period comets. The Solar System itself sits within the Milky Way galaxy, orbiting the galactic centre roughly once every 230 million years. Since the mid-20th century, humans have explored the system directly through space probes, visiting all eight planets, landing on the Moon, and returning samples from comets, asteroids, and the dwarf planets Pluto and Ceres.
