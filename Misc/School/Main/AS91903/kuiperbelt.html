The Kuiper Belt

Pronounced: "Ky-per" belt

Overview

The Kuiper Belt is a circumstellar disc in the outer solar system, extending from the orbit of Neptune at 30 astronomical units out to roughly 50 AU from the Sun. It is similar to the asteroid belt but far larger, about 20 times as wide and 20 to 200 times as massive. While many asteroids are made mostly of rock and metal, most Kuiper Belt objects are composed largely of frozen ices such as methane, ammonia, and water.

Discovery

After Pluto's discovery in 1930, several astronomers speculated it might not be alone out there. In 1943, Kenneth Edgeworth proposed that material beyond Neptune was too widely spaced to form full planets and instead condensed into many smaller bodies, and in 1951 Gerard Kuiper separately speculated about a similar disc, giving the region its eventual name. It wasn't until 1992 that the first Kuiper Belt object since Pluto, later named 15760 Albion, was directly discovered, opening the door to thousands more findings since.

Structure

The main body of the belt runs from about 39.5 AU to 48 AU, shaped heavily by Neptune's gravity through orbital resonances that create gaps much like the Kirkwood gaps in the asteroid belt. Between these resonances lies the classical Kuiper Belt, home to roughly two-thirds of known Kuiper Belt objects, split into a dynamically "cold" population with nearly circular, low-inclination orbits, and a "hot" population with orbits tilted much further from the plane of the solar system.

Composition

Kuiper Belt objects are made of a mix of rock and ices like water, methane, and ammonia, kept solid by temperatures of around 50 K. Their colours range from neutral grey to deep red, a diversity linked to how sunlight chemically alters methane on their surfaces into reddish compounds called tholins. The largest objects, including Pluto, hold onto more volatile ices such as nitrogen and carbon monoxide on their surfaces, while the smallest known objects have only been detected through the way they briefly block the light of a background star.

Dwarf planets

The Kuiper Belt is home to most of the objects astronomers generally accept as dwarf planets, including Pluto, Haumea, Makemake, Quaoar, and Orcus. Pluto is the largest and most massive known member of the belt, though it was reclassified from a planet to a dwarf planet in 2006 after the discovery of other large bodies in the same region raised the question of where a planet's definition should end.

Pluto has a highly eccentric, tilted orbit and shares its 2:3 resonance with Neptune alongside about 200 other objects, together known as plutinos. Haumea is notable for an elongated, fast-spinning shape and two known moons, while Makemake has a reddish surface with hydrocarbons produced by radiation acting on methane ice. Many of these dwarf planets, along with other large Kuiper Belt objects, have their own moons, and their sheer number and similarity to Pluto were part of what led the International Astronomical Union to formally define what a planet is in the first place.

Exploration

The first and so far only spacecraft to explore the Kuiper Belt is New Horizons, launched in 2006. It flew by Pluto on 14 July 2015, revealing detailed views of the dwarf planet and its moons, before going on to fly by the smaller Kuiper Belt object Arrokoth on 1 January 2019, the most distant object ever visited by a spacecraft up to that point.
