Formation of the Solar System

Overview

The formation of the Solar System began about 4.6 billion years ago with the gravitational collapse of a small part of a giant molecular cloud. Most of the collapsing mass collected in the centre, forming the Sun, while the rest flattened into a protoplanetary disk out of which the planets, moons, asteroids, and other small bodies formed. This model, known as the nebular hypothesis, was first developed in the 18th century and has since been refined through advances in astronomy, chemistry, geology, and physics.

The nebular hypothesis

The current standard theory for Solar System formation, the nebular hypothesis, has fallen into and out of favour since being formulated in the 18th century, mainly due to its apparent inability to explain the Sun's relative lack of angular momentum compared to the planets. Since the early 1980s, studies of young stars have shown them to be surrounded by cool discs of dust and gas, exactly as the hypothesis predicts, leading to its re-acceptance.

Formation of the Sun

The nebular hypothesis proposes the Solar System formed from the gravitational collapse of a fragment of a giant molecular cloud, most likely triggered by internal or external forces such as a nearby supernova explosion. As the material within the collapsing nebula condensed, competing forces of gravity, gas pressure, magnetic fields, and rotation caused it to flatten into a spinning protoplanetary disc with a hot, dense protostar at the centre. Within about 50 million years, the temperature and pressure at the Sun's core became great enough for hydrogen fusion to begin, marking its entry into the main sequence phase it remains in today.

Formation of the planets

The planets are thought to have formed through accretion, in which dust grains in orbit around the young Sun collided and gradually built up into larger bodies called planetesimals. In the inner Solar System, temperatures were too warm for volatile compounds to condense, so the terrestrial planets formed from metals and rocky silicates, while the giant planets formed further out beyond the frost line, where icy compounds could remain solid and accumulate into much larger bodies capable of capturing hydrogen and helium.

The asteroid belt

The asteroid belt initially contained more than enough matter to form two to three Earth like planets, but the proximity of Jupiter dramatically altered the region's history after the giant planet formed. Orbital resonances with Jupiter and Saturn scattered many planetesimals or excited their orbits, leaving the asteroid belt with only a small fraction of its original mass, most of it composed of small planetesimals rather than larger bodies.

Planetary migration

According to the nebular hypothesis, Uranus and Neptune may have formed much closer to the Sun, near the orbits of Jupiter and Saturn, before migrating outward to their current positions over hundreds of millions of years. This migration is also thought to explain the structure of the Kuiper belt, as described in the Nice model, in which a resonance between Jupiter and Saturn triggered a period of orbital instability that scattered small icy bodies throughout the outer Solar System.

The Late Heavy Bombardment (LHB)

Gravitational disruption from the outer planets' migration is thought to have sent large numbers of asteroids into the inner Solar System, possibly triggering the Late Heavy Bombardment approximately 4 billion years ago. This period of heavy impacts, if it occurred, lasted several hundred million years and is still evident in the cratering visible on geologically inactive bodies such as the Moon and Mercury.

Formation of moons

Moons are thought to have originated through one of three mechanisms, co-formation from a circumplanetary disc around a giant planet, formation from impact debris, or capture of a passing object. Jupiter and Saturn's large moons likely co-formed from discs around each planet, while Mars's moons Phobos and Deimos are thought to be captured asteroids, and Earth's Moon is thought to have formed from debris thrown into orbit after a large collision with a Mars sized object.

Future evolution

Estimates suggest the current state of the Solar System will remain relatively stable until the Sun exhausts the hydrogen fuel in its core and begins to evolve off the main sequence in around five billion years. As the Sun expands into a red giant, it will likely engulf Mercury and Venus, with Earth's fate less certain, before eventually shedding its outer layers to become a white dwarf orbited by whatever planets remain.

Galactic context

The Solar System orbits the Galactic Center once every 220 to 250 million years, and some scientists have speculated that its path through the galaxy may be linked to periodic mass extinctions on Earth. In about 4 billion years, the Andromeda Galaxy is expected to collide with the Milky Way, though calculations suggest the Sun and its planets are unlikely to be significantly disturbed by the event itself, with the Solar System's ultimate dissolution instead expected to occur gradually over roughly a quadrillion years due to the cumulative effect of passing stars.
