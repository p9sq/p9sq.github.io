Earth | 🜨 or ♁

Overview

Earth is the third planet from the Sun and the only known planet to support life. It has liquid water on its surface, a protective atmosphere, and a magnetic field that shields it from harmful solar radiation. These conditions, combined with its distance from the Sun, make Earth unique among the planets studied so far.

Orbit & Distance

Earth orbits the Sun at an average distance of 150 million km, a distance used as the base unit for the astronomical unit (AU). One orbit takes 365.25 days, which is why an extra day is added every four years in a leap year. Earth sits within the Sun's habitable zone, the region where temperatures allow liquid water to exist on a planet's surface.

Atmosphere

Earth's atmosphere is made up mostly of nitrogen and oxygen, with smaller amounts of argon, carbon dioxide, and other gases. This atmosphere protects the surface from harmful radiation, helps regulate temperature, and provides the oxygen needed for most life. It also burns up small asteroids and debris before they reach the ground.

Surface

About 71% of Earth's surface is covered by water, mostly in the oceans. The remaining land is split into continents and islands, shaped over time by plate tectonics, erosion, and volcanic activity. Earth is the only planet known to have plate tectonics actively reshaping its crust.

Rotation & Day Length

Earth rotates once every 24 hours, which gives us the day and night cycle. Its axis is tilted at about 23.5 degrees, and this tilt is the reason Earth has seasons as it orbits the Sun.

Moon

Earth has one natural satellite, the Moon. It formed around 4.5 billion years ago, likely from debris left after a collision between early Earth and a Mars-sized object. The Moon's gravity is responsible for ocean tides and helps stabilise Earth's axial tilt.

Life

Earth is the only planet confirmed to host life. Conditions like liquid water, a stable climate, and a protective atmosphere allowed simple organisms to form billions of years ago, eventually leading to the diversity of life seen today.

The Moon | ☾ or ☽

Overview

The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite and the fifth largest moon in the Solar System. It is the closest celestial body to Earth, and the only one humans have physically visited. Its gravity shapes ocean tides and helps keep Earth's axial tilt stable over long timescales.

Formation

The Moon likely formed around 4.5 billion years ago, shortly after Earth itself. The leading explanation is the giant impact hypothesis, where a Mars-sized object, often called Theia, collided with early Earth. The collision threw a huge amount of debris into orbit around Earth. Over time, this debris clumped together under gravity, forming the Moon. This explains why the Moon and Earth share similar compositions in their outer layers.

Orbit & Distance

The Moon orbits Earth at an average distance of about 384,400 km. It takes about 27.3 days to complete one orbit around Earth. The Moon is tidally locked to Earth, meaning the same side always faces us. The far side is never visible from Earth's surface.

Surface

The Moon's surface is covered in craters, mountains, and large dark plains called maria, formed by ancient volcanic activity. It has no atmosphere, so there is no wind or weather to erode the surface. This means footprints and equipment left by astronauts can remain undisturbed for a long time.

Phases

The Moon goes through phases as it orbits Earth, caused by changing angles between the Sun, Earth, and Moon. These phases include the new moon, first quarter, full moon, and last quarter. A full cycle through all phases takes about 29.5 days.

Effect on the Earth

The Moon's gravity pulls on Earth's oceans, creating tides. It also helps stabilise the tilt of Earth's axis, which keeps the planet's seasons relatively consistent over long periods of time. Without the Moon, Earth's tilt could vary more dramatically, leading to less stable climate patterns.

Exploration

The Moon is the only body beyond Earth that humans have visited in person. NASA's Apollo missions landed astronauts on its surface between 1969 and 1972, with Apollo 11 being the first crewed landing. Since then, multiple countries have sent uncrewed missions to study the Moon further.
