Saturn | ♄

Overview

Saturn is the sixth planet from the Sun and the second largest in the Solar System, after Jupiter. It is a gas giant, with an average radius of about 9 times that of Earth, and has an eighth of the average density of Earth, but is over 95 times more massive. Even though Saturn is almost as big as Jupiter, it has less than a third of its mass.

Physical characteristics

Saturn is a gas giant, composed predominantly of hydrogen and helium, and lacks a definite surface. It is the only planet in the Solar System that is less dense than water, about 30% less. Saturn's rotation makes it an oblate spheroid, flattened at the poles and bulging at the equator, with an equatorial radius more than 10% longer than the polar radius.

Internal structure

Saturn has a diffuse core, extending 60% of the way to the planet's surface, composed of an indeterminate gradient of rock and ice toward the centre. This is surrounded by a thick layer of liquid metallic hydrogen, then a liquid layer of helium-saturated molecular hydrogen, transitioning gradually to gas as altitude increases. Saturn has a hot interior, reaching 11,700°C at its core, and radiates 2.5 times more energy into space than it receives from the Sun.

Atmosphere

The outer atmosphere of Saturn contains 96.3% molecular hydrogen and 3.25% helium by volume, along with trace amounts of ammonia, acetylene, ethane, propane, phosphine, and methane. Saturn's atmosphere exhibits a banded pattern similar to Jupiter's, though much fainter, and the winds on Saturn are the second fastest among the Solar System's planets, after Neptune's, with peak easterly winds of 500 m/s.

Storms & Polar features

Saturn's usually bland atmosphere occasionally exhibits long-lived ovals and other features, including the Great White Spot, a short-lived phenomenon that occurs once every Saturnian year around the northern hemisphere's summer solstice. A persisting hexagonal wave pattern surrounds the north polar vortex, with sides about 14,500 km long, longer than the diameter of Earth. Saturn's south pole has a warm polar vortex, the only known example of such a phenomenon in the Solar System, along with a hurricane-like storm with a clearly defined eyewall.

Magnetic field

Saturn has an intrinsic magnetic field with a simple, symmetric dipole shape, with a strength at the equator approximately one twentieth of that of Jupiter's field and slightly weaker than Earth's magnetic field. This magnetic field is most likely generated similarly to Jupiter's, by currents in the liquid metallic hydrogen layer, and it is efficient at deflecting solar wind particles.

Rings

Saturn is probably best known for its system of planetary rings, which extend from 6,630 to 120,700 kilometres outward from Saturn's equator and are composed predominantly of water ice, with trace amounts of tholin impurities. There is debate over the age of the rings, with one side supporting that they are as old as Saturn itself, and another proposing they are much younger, formed around 100 million years ago from a destroyed moon nicknamed Chrysalis.

Moons

Saturn has 292 known moons, 63 of which have formal names, along with dozens to hundreds of moonlets in the rings. Titan, the largest moon, comprises more than 90% of the mass in orbit around Saturn, including the rings, and is the only satellite in the Solar System with a substantial atmosphere. Saturn's moon Enceladus has often been regarded as a potential habitat for microbial life, with evidence of liquid water reservoirs erupting in geysers from its south polar region.

Exploration

Pioneer 11 made the first flyby of Saturn in September 1979, followed by Voyager 1 in November 1980 and Voyager 2 in August 1981, both of which sent back detailed images of the planet, its rings, and its moons. The Cassini-Huygens spacecraft entered orbit around Saturn on 1 July 2004, releasing the Huygens probe to land on Titan in January 2005, before Cassini's mission ended with a planned atmospheric entry into Saturn in September 2017.

The Saturnian moons (Mimas, Enceladus, Tethys, Dione, Rhea, Titan, & Iapetus)

Overview

Saturn has 292 moons with confirmed orbits as of April 2026, the most of any planet in the Solar System. Saturn's moons are diverse in size, ranging from tiny moonlets to Titan, which is larger than the planet Mercury and the second-largest moon in the Solar System. Three of these moons possess particularly notable features: Titan has an Earth-like atmosphere and hydrocarbon lakes, Enceladus emits jets of ice from its south polar region, and Iapetus has contrasting black and white hemispheres.

Classification

Twenty-four of the confirmed moons are regular satellites, with prograde orbits not greatly inclined to Saturn's equatorial plane, except Iapetus. The remaining 268 moons are irregular satellites, which have high orbital inclinations and eccentricities mixed between prograde and retrograde, and are probably captured minor planets or fragments from their collisional breakup. These irregular satellites are classified into the prograde Inuit and Gallic groups and the large retrograde Norse group.

Discovery

Before telescopic photography, eight moons of Saturn were discovered by direct observation, starting with Titan in 1655 by Christiaan Huygens. The Cassini mission, arriving in 2004, discovered several small inner moons, and improvements in telescope technology later allowed astronomers to detect dozens of faint, distant irregular moons. Announcements in 2023 and 2025 brought Saturn's total moon count above 100 and then to 274, and further additions in 2026 pushed the number to 292.

Formation

There are three main scenarios proposed to explain how the regular moons of Saturn formed. One proposes they are remnants of a giant impact or the disruption of a previous moon system, another suggests they formed directly from the original circumplanetary disk around Saturn, and a third proposes they coalesced from a previously much more massive set of rings. Saturn's satellite system is very lopsided, with Titan comprising 96% of the mass in orbit around the planet.

Inner & Ring moons

Several small moons orbit closest to Saturn, occupying spaces between gaps or on the edges of the main rings, with some like Pan, Daphnis, and Prometheus acting as shepherd moons. Janus and Epimetheus are co-orbital moons, the only known example of this arrangement in the Solar System, swapping orbits every four years. Trojan moons, a feature unique to the Saturnian system, include Telesto and Calypso, which share Tethys's orbit, and Helene and Polydeuces, which share Dione's orbit.

Major moons

There are seven moons large enough to be ellipsoidal, with Hyperion often included to make eight major moons. Mimas is the smallest and least massive of the round moons, while Enceladus is the smallest body in the Solar System known to be geologically active today. Titan, at 5,149 km in diameter, is the second largest moon in the Solar System and the only one with a dense atmosphere, while Iapetus is known for its unusual two-toned surface, with a pitch-black leading hemisphere and a bright trailing hemisphere.

Irregular moons

The Inuit group includes 39 prograde outer moons split into three subgroups named after their largest members, Kiviuq, Paaliaq, and Siarnaq. The Gallic group includes 19 prograde outer moons, with named members including Albiorix, Bebhionn, Erriapus, and Tarvos. All 210 retrograde outer moons are classified into the Norse group, further split into subgroups including Phoebe, Mundilfari, Kari, and a low-inclination subgroup, with Phoebe itself being by far the largest irregular moon of Saturn.

Moonlets & Rings

Saturn's rings contain moonlets, objects generally too small to be treated as moons or directly imaged, with over 150 detected embedded in the rings by the disturbances they create. In 2022, scientists proposed a hypothetical former moon named Chrysalis, which may have been torn apart by Saturn to form its current ring system. A comet, P/2020 F1, is calculated to have made a close approach to Saturn in 1936, closer than the orbit of Titan, possibly having briefly orbited the planet as a temporary satellite.
