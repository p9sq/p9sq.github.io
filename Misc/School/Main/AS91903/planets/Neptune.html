Neptune | ♆ or ⯉

Overview

Neptune is the eighth and farthest known planet orbiting the Sun. It is the fourth largest planet in the Solar System by diameter, the third most massive planet, and the densest giant planet, at 17 times the mass of Earth. Being composed primarily of gases and liquids, it has no well-defined solid surface, and orbits the Sun once every 164.8 years at a distance of about 4.5 billion km.

Discovery

Neptune is the only planet in the Solar System that was not initially found by direct observation. Unexpected changes in the orbit of Uranus led Alexis Bouvard to hypothesise that an unknown planet was perturbing it, and its position was later mathematically predicted independently by John Couch Adams and Urbain Le Verrier. Neptune was directly observed with a telescope on 23 September 1846 by Johann Gottfried Galle, within a degree of the position Le Verrier had predicted.

Name

Shortly after its discovery, Neptune was referred to simply as the planet exterior to Uranus, with several names proposed, including Janus and Oceanus. Urbain Le Verrier initially proposed naming the planet after himself, but this met stiff resistance outside France, and the name Neptune, after the Roman god of the sea, soon became internationally accepted.

Physical characteristics

Neptune's mass is intermediate between Earth and the larger gas giants, at 17.15 times that of Earth but just one nineteenth that of Jupiter. Its equatorial radius of 24,764 km is nearly four times that of Earth, and like Uranus it is classified as an ice giant due to its smaller size and higher concentration of volatiles compared to Jupiter and Saturn.

Internal structure

Neptune's internal structure resembles that of Uranus, with an atmosphere forming about 5 to 10 percent of its mass, a mantle rich in water, ammonia, and methane equivalent to 10 to 15 Earth masses, and a core likely composed of iron, nickel, and silicates with a mass about 1.2 times that of Earth. At a depth of 7,000 km, conditions may cause methane to decompose into diamond crystals that rain downward.

Atmosphere

At high altitudes, Neptune's atmosphere is 80 percent hydrogen and 19 percent helium, with a trace amount of methane that gives the planet its faint blue hue. The atmosphere is subdivided into a lower troposphere, where temperature decreases with altitude, and a stratosphere, where temperature increases with altitude, with the whole system eventually transitioning into the thermosphere and exosphere.

Weather

Neptune's weather is characterised by extremely dynamic storm systems, with winds reaching speeds of almost 2,200 km/h, the fastest of any planet in the Solar System. In 1989, Voyager 2 discovered the Great Dark Spot, an anticyclonic storm system comparable to Jupiter's Great Red Spot, though it had disappeared by the time Hubble observed the planet in 1994, with a new storm appearing in the northern hemisphere instead.

Magnetic field

Neptune's magnetosphere consists of a magnetic field strongly tilted relative to its rotational axis at 47 degrees and offset from the planet's physical centre, closely resembling the magnetosphere of Uranus. This field may be generated by convective fluid motions in a thin spherical shell of electrically conducting liquids within the planet.

Rings

Neptune has a planetary ring system, though one much less substantial than that of Saturn or Uranus. The three main rings are the Adams, Le Verrier, and Galle rings, with the outermost Adams ring containing five prominent arcs that are thought to be held together by the gravitational effects of the nearby moon Galatea.

Moons

Neptune has 16 known moons, with Triton accounting for more than 99.5% of the mass in orbit around the planet. Unlike other large planetary moons in the Solar System, Triton has a retrograde orbit, suggesting it was captured rather than forming in place, and it is slowly spiralling inward due to tidal forces, eventually to be torn apart in about 28 billion years.

Exploration

Voyager 2 is the only spacecraft that has visited Neptune, making its closest approach on 25 August 1989 before passing close to Triton later the same day. The flyby confirmed the planet's magnetic field, determined its rotation period, and discovered six new moons along with additional rings.

The Neptunian moons (Triton and Proteus)

Overview

There are 16 known moons of the planet Neptune, fourteen of which are named after water deities and creatures in Greek mythology. The largest of them is Triton, discovered by William Lassell just 17 days after the discovery of Neptune itself. Over a century passed before the discovery of the second moon, Nereid, in 1949, and another 40 years passed before Proteus, Neptune's second largest moon, was discovered in 1989.

Discovery

No further moons were found after Nereid until Voyager 2 flew by Neptune in 1989, discovering five inner moons along with rediscovering Larissa. In 2013, Mark Showalter discovered Hippocamp while examining old Hubble images, using a technique to stack multiple images and bring out faint details. The most recent additions came in 2021, when Scott Sheppard and colleagues used the Subaru Telescope to discover two more irregular moons, bringing the total to 16.

Triton

Triton is unique among moons of planetary mass in that its orbit is retrograde to Neptune's rotation and inclined relative to Neptune's equator, suggesting it did not form around Neptune but was gravitationally captured. It is one of the coldest bodies in the Solar System, with a surface temperature of about 38 K, and has a thin atmosphere primarily made of nitrogen with small amounts of methane and carbon monoxide. Because of its retrograde orbit and proximity to Neptune, tidal deceleration is causing Triton to spiral inward, leading to its eventual destruction in about 28 billion years.

Inner moons

Inward of Triton are seven small inner moons, Neptune's only regular satellites, all following prograde orbits close to the planet's equatorial plane. These moons were likely re-accreted from a rubble disc formed after Triton's capture disrupted Neptune's original satellite system. The inner moons are closely associated with Neptune's rings, with Despina possibly shepherding the LeVerrier ring and Galatea helping to confine the arcs within the Adams ring.

Nereid

Nereid is the third largest moon of Neptune and has a prograde but very eccentric orbit, believed to be a former regular satellite scattered into its current orbit during Triton's capture. Spectroscopic observations by the James Webb Space Telescope have shown that Nereid's spectrum differs significantly from minor planets in the outer Solar System, suggesting it may be the only intact original moon of Neptune to have survived Triton's arrival.

Outer irregular moons

Beyond Triton and Nereid, Neptune has seven smaller irregular moons that are more typical of the irregular moons found around other giant planets. These are divided into the prograde Sao group and the retrograde Neso group, with the Neso group moons having the largest orbits of any natural satellites discovered in the Solar System, with orbital periods exceeding 25 years.

Mass distribution

The mass distribution of the Neptunian moons is the most lopsided of any giant planet's satellite system, with Triton making up nearly all of the mass while every other moon combined comprises only about one third of one percent. This lopsidedness is thought to result from Triton's capture disrupting and destroying much of Neptune's original satellite system, with only later re-accretion from the resulting rubble producing the present-day inner moons.
