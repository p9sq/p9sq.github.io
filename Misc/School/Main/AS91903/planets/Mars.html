Mars | ♂

Overview

Mars is the fourth planet from the Sun. It is also known as the Red Planet, for its orange-red appearance. Mars is a desert-like rocky planet with a tenuous atmosphere that is primarily carbon dioxide, retaining some water in the ground and thinly in the atmosphere, but no bodies of liquid surface water.

Physical characteristics

Mars is approximately half the diameter of Earth or twice that of the Moon, with a surface area only slightly less than the total area of Earth's dry land. Mars is less dense than Earth, having about 15% of Earth's volume and 11% of Earth's mass, resulting in about 38% of Earth's surface gravity. The red-orange appearance of the Martian surface is caused by iron oxide and the mineral goethite.

Internal structure

Like Earth, Mars is differentiated into a dense metallic core overlaid by less dense rocky layers. The outermost layer is the crust, averaging about 42 to 56 kilometres thick. Mars's iron and nickel core is at least partially molten, is around half of Mars's radius, and is enriched in light elements such as sulfur, oxygen, carbon, and hydrogen.

Geography

The terrain of Mars roughly follows a north-south divide, the Martian dichotomy, with the northern hemisphere mainly consisting of relatively flat, low-lying plains, and the southern hemisphere of cratered highlands. The planet hosts many enormous extinct volcanoes, including the tallest, Olympus Mons at 21.9 km tall, as well as one of the largest canyons in the Solar System, Valles Marineris, at 4,000 km long.

Atmosphere

The atmosphere of Mars consists of about 96% carbon dioxide, 1.93% argon, and 1.89% nitrogen, along with traces of oxygen and water. The atmosphere is quite dusty, giving the Martian sky a tawny colour when seen from the surface. Mars lost its magnetosphere 4 billion years ago, so the solar wind interacts directly with the Martian ionosphere, stripping atoms from the outer atmosphere.

Water

While Mars contains water in larger amounts, most of it is dust-covered water ice at the Martian polar ice caps. Landforms visible on Mars strongly suggest that liquid water has existed on the planet's surface, including huge outflow channels and dendritic valley networks likely carved by ancient precipitation. Researchers suspect much of the low northern plains may once have been covered with an ocean hundreds of meters deep, though this theory remains controversial.

Orbit & Rotation

Mars's average distance from the Sun is roughly 230 million km, with an orbital period of 687 Earth days. The solar day on Mars is only slightly longer than an Earth day, at 24 hours, 39 minutes, and 35 seconds. The axial tilt of Mars is 25.19 degrees, similar to Earth's, giving Mars seasons much like Earth's, though nearly twice as long.

Moons

Mars has two relatively small natural moons, Phobos and Deimos, which orbit at 9,376 km and 23,460 km from the planet. The origin of both moons is unclear, although a popular theory states they were asteroids captured into Martian orbit. Because the orbit of Phobos is below synchronous altitude, tidal forces are gradually lowering it, and in about 50 million years it could crash into Mars or break up into a ring.

Life

The current understanding of planetary habitability favours planets with liquid water on their surface, and Mars's thin atmosphere prevents this from persisting today. Evidence suggests the planet was once significantly more habitable than it is today, but whether living organisms ever existed there remains unknown. The Cheyava Falls rock, discovered in 2024, has been designated by NASA as a potential biosignature and was sampled by the Perseverance rover for possible return to Earth.

Exploration

The first spacecraft to visit Mars was Mars 1 of the Soviet Union in 1963, though contact was lost en route. NASA's Mariner 4 became the first to successfully transmit data from Mars in 1965, and probes have been active on Mars continuously since 1997. As of 2023, Mars hosts ten functioning spacecraft, with several sample return missions planned for the late 2020s and 2030s.

The Martian moons (Phobos & Deimos)

Overview

The two moons of Mars are Phobos and Deimos. They are irregular in shape. Both were discovered by American astronomer Asaph Hall in August 1877 and are named after the Greek mythological twin characters Phobos and Deimos, who accompanied their father Ares into battle.

Physical characteristics

Compared to Earth's Moon, the moons Phobos and Deimos are very small. Phobos has a diameter of 22.2 km and a mass of 1.08x10^16 kg, while Deimos measures 12.6 km across, with a mass of 1.5x10^15 kg. Phobos orbits closer to Mars, with a semi-major axis of 9,377 km and an orbital period of 7.66 hours, while Deimos orbits farther with a semi-major axis of 23,460 km and an orbital period of 30.35 hours.

Discovery

Asaph Hall discovered Deimos on 12 August 1877 and Phobos on 18 August 1877, at the US Naval Observatory in Washington, D.C. At the time, he was deliberately searching for Martian moons, using the 26-inch refractor telescope then located at Foggy Bottom. The names were suggested by Henry Madan, Science Master of Eton, drawn from Book XV of the Iliad, where Ares summons Fear and Fright.

Appearance from Mars

If viewed from Mars's surface near its equator, a full Phobos would look about one-third as big as a full moon on Earth, while Deimos would look more like a bright star. Speedy Phobos rises in the west, sets in the east, and rises again in just eleven hours, while Deimos, being only just outside synchronous orbit, rises in the east but very slowly, taking 2.7 days to set. There are no total solar eclipses on Mars since the moons are too small to fully cover the Sun, but total lunar eclipses of Phobos happen almost every night.

Orbital fate

Both moons are tidally locked, always presenting the same face towards Mars. Since Phobos orbits Mars faster than the planet rotates, tidal forces are slowly decreasing its orbital radius, and at some point in the future it will be broken up by these forces and either crash into Mars or form a ring. Deimos, on the other hand, is far enough away that its orbit is being slowly boosted instead, similar to Earth's Moon.

Origin

The origin of the Martian moons is still controversial. Phobos and Deimos both have much in common with carbonaceous C-type asteroids, leading to the hypothesis that they may be captured main-belt asteroids. Other hypotheses suggest they formed from material ejected by an impact on Mars that reaccreted in orbit, or that they are remnants of a disrupted common progenitor moon, similar to the leading theory for the origin of Earth's Moon.

Exploration

Two probes under the Soviet Phobos program were launched in 1988, and Phobos 2 successfully photographed Phobos before failing. The Russian Fobos-Grunt probe, intended as the first sample return mission from Phobos, was stranded in Earth orbit in 2011 after a rocket failure. JAXA plans to launch its Martian Moons eXploration mission in 2026 to bring back the first samples from Phobos, with the Return Module expected to arrive back on Earth in July 2029.
