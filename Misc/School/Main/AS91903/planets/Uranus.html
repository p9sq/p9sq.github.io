Uranus | ⛢ or ♅

Overview

Uranus is the seventh planet from the Sun. It is a gaseous cyan coloured ice giant, made mostly of water, ammonia, and methane in a supercritical phase of matter, which astronomy calls ice or volatiles. The planet has a marked axial tilt of 82.23 degrees with a retrograde rotation period of 17 hours and 14 minutes, meaning its poles get around 42 years of continuous sunlight followed by 42 years of continuous darkness.

Discovery

Uranus was observed several times before its recognition as a planet, but was generally misidentified as a star. William Herschel observed Uranus on 13 March 1781 from Bath, England, and initially reported it as a comet, though other astronomers soon suspected it was a planet due to its nearly circular orbit. The discovery doubled the known size of the Solar System, since Uranus is around twice as far from the Sun as Saturn.

Name

The name Uranus references the ancient Greek deity of the sky, known as Caelus in Roman mythology, making it the only planet whose English name derives from Greek rather than Roman mythology. Consensus on the name was not reached until almost 70 years after discovery, and it became universal in 1850 when the last major holdout switched from using the name Georgium Sidus.

Physical characteristics

Uranus has the third-largest diameter and fourth-largest mass among the Solar System's planets, with a mass roughly 14.5 times that of Earth. Its density of 1.27 g per cubic centimetre makes it the second least dense planet after Saturn, indicating it is made primarily of ices such as water, ammonia, and methane.

Internal structure

The standard model of Uranus's structure describes three layers: a rocky core at the centre, an icy mantle in the middle, and an outer gaseous hydrogen and helium envelope. The core has a mass of only 0.55 Earth masses, while the mantle comprises the bulk of the planet at around 13.4 Earth masses. Uranus's internal heat is markedly lower than the other giant planets, and the reason for this remains unclear.

Atmosphere

Uranus's atmosphere consists mainly of molecular hydrogen and helium, with methane as the third most abundant component. Methane's absorption bands in the visible and near-infrared give Uranus its aquamarine or cyan colour. The atmosphere can be divided into three layers, the troposphere, stratosphere, and thermosphere, with no mesosphere in between.

Magnetic field

Uranus's magnetic field is unusual, tilted 59 degrees from its axis of rotation and shifted from the planet's centre by as much as one third of its radius. This unusual geometry results in a highly asymmetric magnetosphere, with field strength varying greatly between hemispheres. Neptune has a similarly displaced and tilted magnetic field, suggesting this may be a common feature of ice giants.

Rings

Uranus has 13 known rings, composed of extremely dark particles ranging in size from micrometres to a fraction of a metre. The rings were definitively discovered in 1977 during a stellar occultation, and were directly imaged when Voyager 2 passed Uranus in 1986. A pair of outer rings was later found by the Hubble Space Telescope in 2005, with one appearing blue and the other red.

Moons

Uranus has 29 known natural satellites, with names chosen from characters in the works of William Shakespeare and Alexander Pope. The five main satellites are Miranda, Ariel, Umbriel, Titania, and Oberon, with Titania being the largest at a radius of about 789 km. The Uranian satellite system is the least massive among the giant planets, and the moons are ice-rock conglomerates composed of roughly 50% ice and 50% rock.

Exploration

Voyager 2 made its closest approach to Uranus on 24 January 1986, coming within 81,500 km of the cloud tops before continuing to Neptune. It remains the only spacecraft to have visited the planet. Plans include China's Tianwen-4 mission, which is set to send a subprobe flying by Uranus in 2045 on its way to interstellar space.

The Uranian moons (Miranda, Ariel, Umbriel, Titania, & Oberon)

Overview

There are 29 known moons of the planet Uranus. The 27 with names are named after characters that appear in William Shakespeare's plays and Alexander Pope's poem The Rape of the Lock. Uranus's moons are divided into three groups: fourteen inner moons, five major moons, and ten irregular moons, with the inner and major moons having prograde orbits while the irregular moons have distant, highly inclined, and mostly retrograde orbits.

Discovery

William Herschel discovered the first two moons, Titania and Oberon, in 1787, six years after discovering the planet itself. The other three ellipsoidal moons were discovered in 1851 by William Lassell and in 1948 by Gerard Kuiper. Uranus was the last giant planet without any known irregular moons until 1997, and the most recent discovery, an inner moon designated Uranus XXVIII, was announced in August 2025 after being spotted in James Webb Space Telescope images.

Naming

Although the first two Uranian moons were discovered in 1787, they were not named until 1852. John Herschel, son of the planet's discoverer, chose to name the moons after magical spirits in English literature rather than Greek mythology, starting with the fairies Oberon and Titania from A Midsummer Night's Dream. Subsequent names have generally focused on Shakespeare's source material rather than continuing the airy spirits theme.

Inner moons

Uranus has 14 known inner moons, whose orbits all lie inside that of Miranda. Some are classified into the Portia group and the Belinda group based on similar orbital distances, and all of them are intimately connected with the rings of Uranus, which probably resulted from the fragmentation of one or several small inner moons. The inner moons constantly perturb each other, and simulations suggest some may eventually collide, with Desdemona and Cressida possibly colliding within the next million years.

Major moons

Uranus has five major moons, Miranda, Ariel, Umbriel, Titania, and Oberon, ranging in diameter from 472 km for Miranda to 1578 km for Titania. All comprise approximately equal amounts of rock and ice, except Miranda, which is made primarily of ice, and their surfaces are heavily cratered, though most show signs of past geological activity such as canyons and, in Miranda's case, unusual racetrack-like structures called coronae. Titania and Oberon may harbour liquid water oceans at the boundary between their rocky cores and icy mantles.

Irregular moons

Uranus's irregular moons range in size from 120 to 200 km for Sycorax down to under 10 km for the smallest known member. The only known group among them is the Caliban group, clustered at similar orbital distances and inclinations, and includes three retrograde moons. Margaret is the only known irregular moon of Uranus with a prograde orbit, and it has one of the most eccentric orbits of any moon in the Solar System.
