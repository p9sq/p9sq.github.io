Jupiter | ♃

Overview

Jupiter is the fifth planet from the Sun, and the largest in the Solar System. It is a gas giant with a mass nearly 2.5 times that of all the other planets in the Solar System combined and slightly less than one thousandth the mass of the Sun. It is the third brightest natural object in Earth's night sky, after the Moon and Venus.

Formation

Jupiter is believed to be the oldest planet in the Solar System, having formed just one million years after the Sun and roughly 50 million years before Earth. Current models suggest Jupiter formed at or beyond the snow line, where temperatures were cold enough for volatiles like water to condense into solids. According to the grand tack hypothesis, Jupiter migrated inward before Saturn's gravitational influence pulled the pair back outward to their current locations, eventually allowing the inner planets, including Earth, to form from the remaining rubble.

Physical characteristics

Jupiter's chemical composition is primarily hydrogen and helium, materials classified as gases in planetary geology. It has a diameter of 142,976 km at its equator, giving it a volume 1,321 times that of Earth, though its average density is lower than any of the four terrestrial planets. Jupiter radiates more heat than it receives from the Sun, due to the Kelvin-Helmholtz mechanism within its contracting interior.

Internal structure

Data from the Juno mission showed that Jupiter has a diffuse core that mixes into its mantle, extending for 30 to 50% of the planet's radius, comprising heavy elements with a combined mass 7 to 25 times that of Earth. Outside this lies a mantle of fluid metallic hydrogen, and further out a transparent interior atmosphere of hydrogen existing in a supercritical fluid state. Temperature and pressure increase steadily inward, with the diluted core estimated at 20,000 K.

Atmosphere

The atmosphere of Jupiter is approximately 76% hydrogen and 24% helium by mass, with trace amounts of carbon, oxygen, sulfur, and neon, as well as ammonia, water vapour, and hydrocarbons. Jupiter is perpetually covered with clouds of ammonia crystals, forming lighter zones and darker belts whose interactions cause storms and turbulence, with wind speeds of 100 metres per second common in the zonal jet streams.

Great Red Spot

A well-known feature of Jupiter is the Great Red Spot, a persistent anticyclonic storm located 22 degrees south of the equator, first observed in 1831 and possibly as early as 1665. The storm is larger than Earth, though it has significantly decreased in size since its discovery, from about 41,000 km across in the late 1800s to around 16,500 by 10,940 kilometres, as of 2015.

Magnetic field

Jupiter's magnetic field is the strongest of any planet in the Solar System, thought to be generated by eddy currents within the fluid, metallic hydrogen core. The interaction of the magnetosphere with the solar wind generates a bow shock at about 75 Jupiter radii from the planet. The four largest moons of Jupiter all orbit within the magnetosphere, which protects them from the solar wind.

Rings

Jupiter has a faint planetary ring system composed of three main segments: an inner torus of particles known as the halo, a relatively bright main ring, and an outer gossamer ring. These rings appear to be made of dust, unlike Saturn's icy rings, with the main ring most likely made from material ejected from the small moons Adrastea and Metis.

Moons

Jupiter has 115 known natural satellites, of which only 16 are larger than 10 km in diameter. The four largest, known as the Galilean moons, are Ganymede, Callisto, Io, and Europa, and are visible from Earth with binoculars. The orbits of Io, Europa, and Ganymede form a pattern known as a Laplace resonance, with tidal flexing from this resonance generating heat that drives the volcanic activity seen on Io.

Exploration

Jupiter has been visited by automated spacecraft since 1973, when Pioneer 10 sent back the first close-up data on its properties. The first spacecraft to orbit Jupiter was the Galileo mission, arriving in 1995, and NASA's Juno mission has been studying the planet from a polar orbit since 2016. Newer missions, including the European Space Agency's Jupiter Icy Moon Explorer and NASA's Europa Clipper, are now investigating Jupiter's icy moons for signs of subsurface oceans.

The Jovian moons (Io, Europa, Ganymede, & Callisto)

Overview

There are 115 known moons of the planet Jupiter as of April 2026. All together, Jupiter's moons form a satellite system, colloquially referred to as the Jovian system. The most massive of the moons are the four Galilean moons, Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto, all of which were independently discovered in 1610 by Galileo Galilei and Simon Marius and were the first objects found to orbit a body that was neither Earth nor the Sun.

Classification

Of Jupiter's moons, eight are regular satellites with prograde and nearly circular orbits that are not greatly inclined with respect to Jupiter's equatorial plane. The remainder of Jupiter's moons are outer irregular satellites whose prograde and retrograde orbits are much farther from Jupiter and have high inclinations and eccentricities. The largest of these were likely asteroids captured from solar orbits by Jupiter, before impacts with other small bodies shattered them into fragments, forming collisional families of moons sharing similar orbits.

Inner moons

The inner satellites, or Amalthea group, consist of Metis, Adrastea, Amalthea, and Thebe, which orbit very close to Jupiter. These moons, along with several of unseen inner moonlets, replenish and maintain Jupiter's faint ring system, with Metis and Adrastea helping to maintain the main ring, and Amalthea and Thebe each maintaining their own faint outer rings.

Galilean moons

The main group, known as the Galilean moons, consists of Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto. They are among the largest objects in the Solar System outside the Sun and the eight planets, containing approximately 99.997% of the total mass in orbit around Jupiter. Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto are suspected of having subsurface water oceans, and Io may have a subsurface magma ocean, with tidal flexing from a 1:2:4 orbital resonance driving heat generation in the moons' interiors.

Irregular moons

The irregular satellites are substantially smaller objects with more distant and eccentric orbits, forming families that share similarities in orbit and composition. Prograde families include the Himalia group and the Carpo group, while retrograde families include the Carme, Ananke, and Pasiphae groups. These families are believed to be at least partially collisional families, created when larger parent bodies were shattered by impacts from asteroids captured by Jupiter's gravitational field.

Discovery

The first certain observations of Jupiter's satellites were made by Galileo Galilei in 1609, and by January 1610 he had sighted the four Galilean moons. No additional satellites were discovered until E. E. Barnard observed Amalthea in 1892, after which further discoveries followed through photographic plates and later digital cameras. From 2000 onward, surveys led by astronomers including Scott Sheppard rapidly expanded the known moon count, reaching 115 as of April 2026, with many more irregular moons expected to be found by upcoming deep sky surveys.

Naming

The Galilean moons were named by Simon Marius soon after their discovery, though these names fell out of favour until becoming popular again in the mid-20th century. The practice since 1975 has been to name newly discovered moons after lovers and favourites of the god Jupiter, or since 2004, also after their descendants, with names ending in "a" or "o" given to prograde satellites and names ending in "e" given to retrograde ones.

Exploration

Nine spacecraft have visited Jupiter, beginning with Pioneer 10 in 1973 and Pioneer 11 a year later. The Voyager probes discovered volcanic activity on Io and water ice on Europa's surface in 1979, while the Galileo spacecraft, arriving in 1995, found evidence for liquid water beneath the surfaces of Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto. More recently, the Juno spacecraft has conducted close flybys of the Galilean moons since 2021, including passes of Ganymede, Europa, and Io.
