Equations and sources

The right sphere shows the map under your star's light. Colour is albedo times a blackbody spectrum at the star's temperature, integrated against the CIE 1931 observer in linear light, with the Sun as white. Brightness is the star's visible flux at the planet, L / (4 pi a^2 sqrt(1 - e^2)) times the blackbody luminous efficacy ratio to the Sun, relative to Earth's 1361 W/m^2. Eye adaptation scales it by flux^0.25 (a mild Stevens' law with partial dark adaptation), then a soft shoulder rolls off highlights without clipping the colour. The map itself stays in Sun-like light. Real clouds also absorb by wavelength, so a texture is only a three-band estimate of this.

2:1 output is true equirectangular. 16:9 compresses longitude to 8/9 of the true width. The sphere preview undoes this.

Kepler: P^2 = 4 pi^2 a^3 / (G (Ms + Mp)) Stefan-Boltzmann: L = 4 pi Rs^2 sigma Ts^4 Equilibrium: Teq = Ts sqrt(Rs / 2a) (1 - Ab)^(1/4) Effective: Te = (Teq^4 + Tint^4)^(1/4) Gravity: g = G Mp / Rp^2 Scale height: H = k Te / (mu mH g) Rossby: Ro = U / (2 Omega Rp) Rhines bands: N = sqrt(2 Omega Rp / U)

Band count follows the Rhines scale (Rhines 1975, Williams 1978). Jupiter gives about 22 bands, Neptune about 5.

Class and subtype follow the thresholds in the config object CFG. Gas giants also get a Sudarsky class (I to V) from effective temperature. Cloud decks follow Sudarsky, Burrows and Hubeny 2000 and Lodders 2003 condensation chemistry, tested at 1 bar. Species outside a 0.55 to 1.0 window of temperature ratio count as buried or absent.

Colour anchors come from Voyager and Cassini imagery of the four solar giants, Evans et al. 2013 for HD 189733 b (blue, geometric albedo 0.40 at 450 nm), Bell et al. 2017 for WASP-12 b (albedo below 0.1), and Kreidberg et al. 2014 for GJ 1214 b (flat spectrum, cloudy).

Haze optical depth scales with sqrt(g0 / g), a temperature term, weak internal heat, and geometric albedo above the expected value. Noise runs on 3D sphere points, so the left and right edges tile and the poles do not pinch.

Composition starts from solar abundances (Asplund et al. 2009) scaled by a metal enrichment that falls with mass, then applies temperature chemistry: CH4 to CO near 700 to 1400 K, NH3 to N2, condensate depletion of H2O, NH3 and H2S, thermal dissociation, and alkali, TiO and silicate vapour in hot atmospheres. Values are mole fractions in units of 0.00000001 percent, so the total is exactly 100.

Circumbinary mode sums the mass and luminosity of both stars. The effective radius joins both surface areas and the effective temperature follows from the summed luminosity. This holds when the planet orbits far outside the pair.

Storm and cloud features follow published observations. Tidally locked planets keep clouds on the nightside and western dayside, as in Parmentier 2016 and the JWST map of LTT 9779 b. Red spot style storms get their length from vortex strength against jet shear, and their shade from UV exposure at the cloud top. Dark spots on ice giants carry bright methane companions. Strong jet gas giants host a Saturn style convective storm. Uranus style planets get a faint dark spot near 25 N and bands near 45 degrees. Below 950 K a hydrocarbon haze thickens and browns. Tidally locked planets cool enough to redistribute heat keep faint bands and show clouds on the nightside and morning limb.

Obliquity raises polar haze depth. Eccentricity shifts Teq by (1 - e^2)^(-1/8) when optional orbital parameters are on. The other optional parameters do not change the map.

Polar polygons follow Rossby-wave theory on a strong polar jet: the stationary wavelength is 2 pi sqrt(U / beta_eff), with beta_eff = beta + U / sigma^2, and the polygon number is the jet circumference over that wavelength (Morales-Juberias et al., Barbosa Aguiar et al. 2010). Saturn's jet gives 6. A narrower jet gives more sides, as in the decagon near 63 S (Sanchez-Lavega et al., Science Advances). Weaker polar jets give rings of cyclones, as on Jupiter (8 north, 5 south). Ice giants get a single polar vortex.

Brown dwarf mode has no host star. You enter mass and age. Effective temperature follows the Burrows et al. 2001 late-time cooling fit, Teff = 1550 K (t / 1 Gyr)^-0.32 (M / 0.05 solar masses)^0.83, softly capped at 3200 K for young objects. Radius comes from an approximate mass and age fit to published cooling tracks. Enter a measured temperature or radius and it replaces the fit. Below 13 Jupiter masses (the deuterium limit) the object is a sub-brown dwarf. Above 75 Jupiter masses (the hydrogen limit) it is a star.

Spectral class follows effective temperature: M above 2300 K, L down to 1400 K, the L/T transition near 1200 to 1400 K, T down to 500 K, Y below. Cloud decks follow Morley et al. 2012 and 2014: iron and silicate in L dwarfs, sulfide and chloride clouds in T dwarfs, water clouds in the coldest Y dwarfs. Clouds break into patches at the L/T transition. Luhman 16 B and SIMP 0136+0933 show such patchy clouds. Sulfides form below about 900 K, Na2S peaks near 600 K and water condenses near 350 K. Jet speeds of 150 to 300 m/s sit at the top of the Showman and Kaspi 2013 range in rotational light curves. Bands follow the same Rhines scaling as the planets. The visible colours are estimates, since few measurements exist at these wavelengths. Below 350 K the object takes the cold-giant look, with water clouds, ochre bands and storms. WISE 0855 has water features strikingly similar to Jupiter and no measured visible colour. Warm objects add a red thermal glow.

Planet mode treats a mass of 13 to 75 Jupiter masses as a star-bound brown dwarf. It uses the brown dwarf cooling fit for internal temperature and radius, and the brown dwarf colours and clouds. A tick box lets you mark the object as planet-formed instead. GJ 504 b is the case in point. Its mass fits 20 to 30 Jupiter masses with a metallicity of [M/H] near 0.67, which tentatively supports planet-like formation (JWST-TST High Contrast study).

The composition list covers 60 or more species. Atoms and ions of 24 elements use the solar abundances of Asplund et al. 2009. Each element enters the gas when its condensates evaporate, and ionises along a Saha-style ramp set by its first ionisation potential. Cool gas adds PH3, HCl, HF, GeH4, HD and more hydrocarbons. Hot gas adds metal hydrides (FeH, CrH, MgH, CaH), AlO, and the dissociation products of water, CO, N2 and H2S, plus H-, H+ and free electrons. Lithium drops by 99 percent above about 65 Jupiter masses, from the 0.06 to 0.065 solar mass lithium limit (Magazzu et al. 1993, Rebolo et al. 1996). Release temperatures for Mg, Al, Ca, Sc, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn, Ga, Ge, Rb, Sr, Y, Zr and Ba use 50 percent condensation temperatures from FastChem equilibrium condensation at 1 bar. FastChem matches Lodders 2003 within 50 K at 1e-4 bar. Ionisation uses the Saha equation at 3 mbar for planets and 1 bar for brown dwarfs. Jupiter anchors: PH3 0.5 to 1.4 ppm, GeH4 0.58 ppb, D/H 2.6e-5. Hydrides, oxides, dissociation products, HCl, HF, HCN and KCl were set to match FastChem 4.0 gas-phase runs (Stock et al. 2018) at solar abundances for 500 to 4000 K. Those runs have no condensation, so the release gates still decide when each element enters the gas. The hydrocarbons are scaled to Jupiter's stratosphere. ISO gives C2H6 near 5 ppm and C2H2 near 0.14 ppm at 3 mbar, a C3H4 column of 1.5e15 per cm2 (about 5e-9), a C4H2 upper limit near 7e-10, and C6H6 near 3e-10 (Fouchet et al. 2000, Bezard et al. 2001). C2H4 has an equatorial ISO column of 6e14 per cm2 (about 6e-10 to 6e-9), which fits the value used (Bezard et al. 2001). C3H8 is undetected, with upper limits near 10 to 30 ppb at 10 mbar in the north polar region and 8.4e16 per cm2 at the equator, so the value used is only consistent with those limits. CO2 follows Moses et al. 2013: below 1 ppm at solar metallicity and rising steeply with metallicity. FeH is firm in L dwarfs but only tentative in hot Jupiters. SpaceEngine mode keeps only its known gases.

Mass to radius fill uses Chen and Kipping 2017. Hot planets use KCl, ZnS, Na2S and silicate decks beyond the five core species.
