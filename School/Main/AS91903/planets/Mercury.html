Mercury | ☿

Overview

Mercury is the smallest (0.055% as massive as Earth and 0.383% of Earth's radius) and innermost planet in the Solar System. It has no atmosphere to speak of, a heavily cratered surface, and some of the most extreme temperature swings of any planet. Its slow rotation and fast orbit combine to create an unusual relationship between its day and year.

Orbit & Distance

Mercury is the closest planet to the Sun and the smallest planet in the Solar System. It orbits the Sun at an average distance of 57.91 million km, completing one orbit every 88 Earth days. Despite being closest to the Sun, Mercury is not the hottest planet. That title goes to Venus, because Mercury has almost no atmosphere to trap heat. Mercury is also the only planet in the Solar System to have a mild eccentricity of 0.21. Eccentricity is how oval-shaped an object's orbit is. An eccentricity of 0 is a perfect circle, and an eccentricity above one is not an orbit.

Surface

Mercury's surface looks a lot like the Moon. It is covered in craters from billions of years of asteroid and comet impacts. With no real atmosphere to burn up incoming objects or erode old craters, the surface stays scarred for a long time. The largest impact site, the Caloris Basin, stretches about 1550 km across.

Temperature

Temperature swings on Mercury are extreme. During the day, surface temperatures reach around 430 °C. At night, they drop to about -180°C. This happens because Mercury has no atmosphere to hold onto heat or spread it around the planet.

Rotation & Day Length

Mercury rotates slowly. One full rotation on its axis takes 59 Earth days. Combined with its 88-day orbit, a single day on Mercury, from sunrise to sunrise, lasts about 176 Earth days. This is due to a 3:2 spin-orbit resonance, where Mercury rotates three times for every two orbits around the Sun.

Core

Mercury has a large iron core, which makes up about 75% of the planet's radius. This is a bigger proportion than any other planet in the Solar System. Scientists think an early collision with another object may have stripped away much of Mercury's outer rock layers, leaving the core to make up more of the planet.

Moons

Mercury has no moons. Its weak gravity and closeness to the Sun's gravitational pull make it hard for any moon to stay in a stable orbit.

Exploration

Two spacecraft have visited Mercury so far. Mariner 10 flew past it in the 1970s. MESSENGER orbited the planet from 2011 to 2015, mapping its surface and gathering data on its composition and magnetic field.
